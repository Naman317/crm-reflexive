[P010] Abstract—Language agents that call tools tend to repeat actions that have already failed. The problem is sharpest when the lesson is a prohibition (“do not call this endpoint”) rather than a preference, and published benchmark results suggest that frontier models handle prohibitions far worse than preferences. We describe Contrastive Reflexive Memory (CRM), a small runtime layer that stores each failure as a reflex rule (a trigger embedding, the forbidden action and a verified safe alternative) and checks every incoming query against the stored triggers with one matrix-vector product. A matching query receives either a short contrastive directive in front of the prompt (CRM-Prompt) or a logit penalty on the forbidden tokens (CRM-Mask). We test on a 200-task suite modeled on ImplicitMemBench, using a local Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct model. The router itself works well: at a threshold of 0.35 it flags 98.7% of 300 paraphrased triggers and none of 100 benign prompts, and a lookup takes a median of 17 ms on a laptop CPU, almost all of it embedding time. The generation side is much weaker. On 160 test tasks CRM-Prompt reaches 10.2% first-try accuracy, above plain generation (3.3%) but below a retrieval baseline that prepends stored notes (19.4%), and our Ollama version of CRM-Mask scored 1.9%. We report these negative results in full, together with the limits of our runs, and discuss what a stronger test would need.

[P011] I. INTRODUCTION

[P012] Software agents built on large language models act by calling external tools one after another [5], [6]. To be useful over a long session, an agent has to learn from its bad actions and avoid repeating them [4]. An engineer who gets a permission error or a deadlock from some API stops calling it that way almost at once. Current language models seem not to. Over a long conversation they lose negative constraints.

[P013] Memory systems such as Mem0 [2] and MemGPT [3] are good at pulling facts out of past dialogue. They are less suited to prohibitions. A stored note saying that something failed is just more context, and it competes with everything else in the prompt. Fig. 1 shows the gap reported for published models: accuracy on negative inhibition is far below accuracy on positive preference. Our own small-model runs, also in Fig. 1, are low on both.

[P015] Fig. 1. First-try accuracy on negative (inhibition) and positive (preference) tasks. The four left groups are our runs on classical tasks with Qwen2.5-3B; the right groups are published results for larger models [1]. Our local numbers are low for every method and are not directly comparable with the published ones (Section VII).

[P019] We built two ways to act on a rule, CRM-Prompt for black-box models and CRM-Mask for open-weight models. Queries are embedded with a small sentence transformer (384 dimensions), and when the similarity score reaches 0.35 the reflex fires. CRM-Prompt then puts a short directive in front of the user query. CRM-Mask is meant to lower the logits of the forbidden tool names before sampling. We evaluate both on a 200-task suite modeled on ImplicitMemBench [1]. Our contributions are modest, and we state them as they are:

[P020] We formulate failure memory as a buffer of reflex rules with a one-step cosine router, and we give both a prompt-level and a logit-level way to act on a match.

[P021] We measure the router carefully. At τ=0.35 it reaches 98.7% recall on 300 paraphrased triggers with 0.0% false inhibition on 100 benign prompts, and a lookup costs a median of 17 ms on a laptop CPU.

[P022] We report negative results for the generation side. With a 3B model, prompt-level injection helped only a little over plain generation, did worse than a simple retrieval baseline, and our CRM-Mask implementation did not work. We list the weaknesses of these runs in Section VII.

[P038] C. Why Declarative Notes May Fail

[P039] A vector store retrieves facts well, but it treats a prohibition as one more passage to be ranked and read. We think two things can go wrong, and we did not isolate either one in our experiments. When the test query is paraphrased, its similarity to the stored note can drop enough that the note is not retrieved. When it is retrieved, it sits far from the generation point, and under multi-head self-attention the weight on distant tokens tends to shrink as the distraction grows [8]. Next-token prediction is also generative by nature. Pretrained representations tie a concept such as “user analytics API” to the tokens seen most often in training, such as “api_v1”, and suppressing a strong prior may take more than a passive note. CRM therefore drops text retrieval and uses a cosine routing score together with a directive placed right before generation, as described in Section III.

[P040] III. CONTRASTIVE REFLEXIVE MEMORY ARCHITECTURE

[P041] CRM pulls execution failures out of system feedback and error logs. Where declarative systems store conversation text in an external vector store, CRM works as a small runtime circuit that holds a buffer of failure reflexes in local memory. It has three modules in sequence: failure extraction, semantic routing and inhibitory actuation.

[P042] A. Stage 1: Conditioned Failure Extractor

[P043] The idea comes from conditioned inhibition in classical psychology [9], [10]. Rather than saving long text notes, we save a short embedding of the user prompt. When a tool fails, the system records the failure stimuli listed in (4), and the trigger prompt is mapped to the embedding in (5). Each rule is built from three parts:

[P044] Trigger Prompt Parsing (𝒫CS,i): the raw user instruction that came just before the prohibited tool call.

[P045] Prohibited Tool Tagging (ainhibit,i): the exact tool identifier and argument signature that caused the failure, marked for suppression.

[P046] Verified Fallback Assignment (asafe,i): the safe alternative tool or parameter setting that the environment feedback supplied.

[P064] CRM-Prompt is meant for black-box models and CRM-Mask for open-weight models. CRM-Prompt puts an instruction in front of the user prompt, as in (9). [P071] IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

[P072] A. Task Suite

[P073] We built a 200-task suite modeled on ImplicitMemBench [1]. It is our own re-creation and not the official release, so absolute numbers should not be compared with published ones. The suite has four parts:

[P074] Classical Conditioning (100 tasks): positive preference and negative inhibition tasks, covering API version restrictions, file and directory permissions, preferred communication tone and security boundaries.

[P075] Procedural Memory (100 tasks): multi-step habits such as reversed argument syntax, custom session tokens and non-standard output formats.

[P076] Paraphrase probes (300): three versions of each classical trigger, exact, lexical (synonym substitution) and structural (syntactic inversion).

[P077] Benign controls (100): developer and general queries that should not trigger any rule, used to measure false inhibition.

[P078] We set aside 40 tasks as a development set for choosing the directive wording and report results on the other 160. The two sets do not overlap.

[P079] B. Systems and Inference Setup

[P080] We compare four systems. Vanilla gives the model the conversation and nothing else. The Mem0-style baseline stores the feedback as a text note in a vector store [2], [3] and prepends the top three retrieved notes to the prompt. CRM-Prompt prepends the directive when the router score is at least 0.35. CRM-Mask is the stop-sequence version described above [12].

[P081] Everything ran locally through Ollama on a laptop CPU with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, greedy decoding (T=0.0) and a 128-token limit. Each test prompt follows 8 unrelated distractor turns, except in the horizon experiment. Queries are embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2. The main benchmark uses three seeds, and the paraphrase and horizon runs use one. All outputs in the logs come from real model calls.

[P106] C. Router Sensitivity and False Inhibition

[P107] The router is the part of CRM that works well. We swept τ over 300 paraphrased probes and 100 benign prompts (Table V and Fig. 4). At τ=0.15 almost everything fires, with a false inhibition rate (FIR) of 99.0%. At τ=0.35 recall is 98.7% (99% exact, 99% lexical, 98% structural) and FIR is 0.0%, which gives precision 100.0% and F1 99.3%. Above 0.35 recall falls, to 57.7% at 0.65. FIR is already 10.0% at τ=0.30, so the usable range is narrow and the 100 benign prompts are a small sample. This test measures routing only. It does not show that the downstream model then behaves correctly.

[P114] Routing is cheap. With 100 stored rules, a full lookup took a median of 17.2 ms (95th percentile 26.2 ms) over 1,000 queries on a laptop CPU. Nearly all of that is the embedding step (median 17.2 ms). The matrix-vector product alone took a median of 16.5 μs.

[P115] D. Directive Wording on the Development Set

[P116] We tried five plain-text directives on the 40 development tasks, with no distractor turns (Table VI). The intervals overlap heavily, and “Execution Rule: Avoid X. Call Y directly.” (candidate 3) had the lowest score. It is the wording that was used for the test runs in Table II. The development winner was candidate 5, and we did not run it on the test set. The test numbers for CRM-Prompt may therefore understate what a better-chosen directive would give.

[P119] VI. RELATED WORK

[P120] A. Agent Memory and Episodic Retrieval Systems

[P121] Agent frameworks today mostly handle memory through episodic context stores and natural language reflection [2], [3], [4]. Reflexion [4] and ReAct [5] ask the model to look at its own execution failures and add a written post-mortem to the dialogue. This helps within one reasoning chain, but it can fade once the conversation moves through many unrelated interactions.

[P122] Retrieval-augmented systems such as Mem0 [2] and MemGPT [3] keep information over the long term by splitting past dialogue into dense vector indices. Qin et al. [1] argued with ImplicitMemBench that this works poorly for negative constraints, which are stored as passive statements and have to compete with the token probabilities of the model [8].

[P123] B. Constrained Decoding and Policy Steering

[P124] Alignment methods usually change model weights, through reinforcement learning from human feedback or through Direct Preference Optimization [7]. Toolformer [6] trains models to call external tools. Offline updates like these give strong default behavior, but they cannot follow changes that appear at runtime, such as a revoked API key. Inference-time steering leaves the weights alone, as in few-shot prompting [11] and constrained decoding for open-weight models [12]. CRM follows this line for tool-using agents.

[P125] C. Biologically Inspired Conditioned Inhibition

[P126] CRM draws on Pavlovian conditioning and the Rescorla-Wagner model of associative learning [9], [10]. In a nervous system, conditioned inhibition lets an organism suppress a reflex that has been paired with a learned inhibitory stimulus. We map the conditioned stimulus (𝒫CS) and the failure feedback (uUS) onto routing tuples. This is an analogy for design purposes and not a claim about mechanism.

[P127] VII. LIMITATIONS

[P128] Our evidence is narrower than the architecture deserves, and the following points should be kept in mind when reading the results:

[P129] Task suite: the 200 tasks are our own re-creation of ImplicitMemBench, and the published numbers in Table I come from a different setup. Our results cannot be compared directly with them.

[P130] Model and scale: we used one small model (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) on a CPU. The local baselines score low, so we cannot say how CRM behaves with larger models.

[P131] Incomplete runs: the paraphrase and horizon experiments cover 80 and 30 tasks with one seed, and the M=120 condition did not finish. We computed no retention rate. CRM-Prompt matched vanilla tier for tier in the paraphrase run, and we did not find out why.

[P132] CRM-Mask: our version used stop sequences and prefix interception, not logit editing, and it scored 1.9%. A real logit-level test, for example with Hugging Face transformers, is still missing.

[P133] Directive choice: the wording used in the test runs scored lowest on the development set, and the development runs had no distractor turns. The development winner was not run on the test set.

[P134] Scoring: success is a string match against the expected safe call. A correct answer in other words counts as a failure, and we did not audit a sample by hand.

[P135] Router evidence: the benign set has 100 prompts, FIR rises to 10.0% at τ=0.30, and the probes were built from the same tasks as the stored triggers. Real deployment traffic may behave differently. Routing cost grows as 𝒪(KD) with the number of rules, and large stores would need approximate nearest neighbor search.

[P136] VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

[P137] We described Contrastive Reflexive Memory, a layer that turns tool failures into reflex rules and routes each new query against them with one matrix-vector product. The routing half held up well in our tests: 98.7% recall on paraphrased triggers with no false inhibition on benign prompts at τ=0.35, at a cost of tens of microseconds for the product and about 17 ms with embedding. The generation half did not. On a 3B model, prompt-level injection improved on plain generation but not on a simple retrieval baseline, and our mask variant did not work in the form we could run.

[P138] The next steps are clear. We would run real logit masking, test the development-winning directive on the full test set with all three seeds and the paraphrase and horizon runs at full size, add a hand-audited sample of responses, and try larger models. We also want a scorer that recognizes the safe call when it is written in other words. Until then, the router is the result we trust, and the claim that CRM closes the negative-association gap remains unproven.
